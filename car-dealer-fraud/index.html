Car Dealer Fraud in Houston, TX

We review dealer records, purchase papers, mileage, and vehicle disclosures.

Call (832) 981-4988 Ask a question

Used-car purchases in Houston can raise concerns when the vehicle on the driveway does not match the vehicle described in the listing, on the sales lot, or in the paperwork. A buyer may later find accident damage, prior rental use, a title issue, an odometer concern, or equipment that was represented differently before the purchase agreement was signed. We review these concerns for people in Houston, West University Place, Southside Place, and Bellaire.

Some dealer-fraud concerns start with a mechanical problem that appears shortly after delivery. Others begin when a repair shop identifies old collision work, water damage, warning-light history, or a fault that was not discussed during the sale. The important documents can include the advertisement, buyer's order, purchase or lease agreement, odometer disclosure, vehicle-history report, repair orders, and text messages with dealership staff.

This is usually worth looking at now rather than setting aside, because online listings, sales messages, condition photographs, and dealership records can become harder to locate after the transaction. We can compare the documents while the vehicle condition, mileage, and repair history are still easier to trace.

Mileage mismatch and added charges

People often call us saying, “The mileage does not match what I was told,” “They said it had no accident damage,” or “The contract has charges I did not agree to.” Other common complaints include a vehicle advertised with a feature that is missing, a prior title brand discovered after the sale, or a dashboard warning light that returns after the dealership said the issue had been repaired. A buyer may also report several trips back to the dealership for the same transmission, engine, electrical, brake, or air-conditioning problem.

The words may sound similar from the driveway, but the records behind them can point in different directions. An odometer disclosure, an advertisement screenshot, a repair order, a financing form, and a text message each answer a different question about what was said, what was signed, and what condition the vehicle was in when it changed hands.

Case file review

When someone brings a dealer-fraud concern to us, we begin with a case file review. In the first conversation, we usually sort out the basic transaction: whether the vehicle was bought or leased, what concern appeared first, who made the representation, and whether the dispute involves the sales paperwork, the vehicle condition, the mileage, or repair history. We also ask where the vehicle was purchased and whether it has gone back to the dealer or another repair shop.

We then read the purchase or lease agreement, buyer's order, odometer disclosure, warranty papers, and any addendum attached to the sale documents. We gather repair orders and compare the complaint written at check-in with the technician notes, mileage, dates in and out of the shop, diagnostic codes, and parts listed on the invoice. Correspondence with the dealer, including email, text messages, and service department messages, can help establish the sequence of statements and repairs.

The first conversation can settle the broad outline of the concern and identify missing records. Questions about a particular charge, disclosure, repair visit, or statement made during the sale usually need the paperwork in front of us before we can assess how the documents fit together.

Repair attempts, service days, warranty

The number of repair attempts matters when the vehicle has been returned for the same defect or closely related symptoms. Separate repair orders showing a repeated check-engine light, stalling condition, water leak, brake concern, electrical failure, or transmission complaint provide a different record from a single verbal report at the service counter. We look for the mileage at each visit, the customer complaint, the diagnosis, and whether the repair order says the problem was duplicated, repaired, or could not be found.

Days out of service can also matter when the vehicle stayed at a dealership or repair facility while a part was ordered, a diagnosis was repeated, or a repair did not resolve the condition. We compare the drop-off and pickup dates with loaner paperwork, work orders, and messages from the service department. If a fault was reported while warranty coverage was still running, the warranty booklet, repair order date, and mileage entry can all be relevant to the record.

The purchase or lease arrangement, the written disclosures provided at sale, and who retains the original records can affect how clearly the transaction can be reconstructed. A buyer with an advertisement, signed agreement, repair orders, and correspondence has a more complete starting file than someone relying only on memory, but we can still identify which documents are worth locating before taking the next step.

VIN and purchase agreement

Before calling, locate the purchase or lease agreement and the VIN from the windshield, registration, insurance card, or sales paperwork. The agreement and buyer's order usually show the vehicle description, sale date, listed mileage, trade-in terms, add-ons, and signatures. Keep the odometer disclosure, any vehicle-history report, and screenshots of the original online advertisement if they are still available.

Repair orders should show the date the vehicle entered and left the shop, the mileage, the stated complaint, and the technician's findings. Save emails and text messages with the salesperson, finance office, or service department because a message may preserve a statement not shown on the contract.
