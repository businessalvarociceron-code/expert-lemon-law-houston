Truck Lemon Law in Houston, TX

We review recurring truck defects, repair visits, and warranty records in Houston.

Call (832) 981-4988 Ask a question

People call us when a truck "keeps going into limp mode," "won't start after it sits," or "has been back to the shop too many times." Those are the words owners use after a dashboard warning light, transmission shift, electrical failure, or engine noise disrupts work, school runs, towing, or a regular drive across Houston. We translate the description into the repair history, warranty record, and vehicle concern that may need a closer legal review.

Another caller may say the dealer cannot duplicate the problem, even though the truck stalled on the road, lost power under load, or displayed the same warning message more than once. We listen for the practical sequence: what happened in the cab, what the service writer put on the repair order, what part was inspected or replaced, and whether the problem returned after pickup.

A recurring truck defect is worth looking at now rather than leaving in a folder because the connection between the original complaint, the mileage, the warranty period, and each shop visit can become harder to trace as more repair orders and symptoms accumulate.

Warning light and no start

Calls about truck problems often begin with a check-engine light that returns after a repair, a reduced-power message during acceleration, or an engine that shakes, stalls, or loses power while pulling a trailer. We also hear about transmissions that slam into gear, hesitate before moving, surge at highway speed, or slip after the truck has warmed up. A driver may report that the truck will not start, drains the battery overnight, loses its backup camera, or has a screen and controls that stop responding.

Other repair orders involve water entering the cab, a tailgate that will not latch, brakes that feel inconsistent, steering vibration, or an air-conditioning system that stops cooling. The same truck may have several complaints, but we keep the warning light, the no-start event, the transmission concern, and the water leak separate when we read the service record. Problems that sound similar from a driveway can lead to very different file questions once the diagnosis, repair attempt, and mileage entry are on paper.

Truck file review

When someone brings a truck matter to us, we begin with a file review. In the first conversation, we usually establish the basic vehicle history: whether the truck was purchased or leased, the general defect, where repairs occurred, and whether the concern was reported during the warranty period. We also identify the documents already available, such as repair orders, a purchase or lease agreement, warranty correspondence, and messages with the dealer.

The paperwork lets us examine the sequence more closely. We read the purchase or lease agreement, then place each repair order in date order and compare the customer complaint, technician findings, diagnosis, parts listed, mileage, and dates the truck entered and left the shop. We check correspondence with the dealer against the written service record and note when a repair order says a condition could not be duplicated, when a part was replaced, or when the same concern returned. If a document is missing, we explain what record may be useful to locate or request before we assess the truck file further.

Repair attempts and warranty dates

The number of repair attempts matters, but the wording on those repair orders matters too. A truck owner may describe a repeated loss of power, while one invoice says “engine light” and another lists a sensor, fuel system, or transmission diagnosis. We compare the symptom, warning message, technician notes, and part replacement to see whether the shop visits may relate to the same underlying concern or to separate issues.

Days out of service can also matter when the truck remained at a dealership for diagnosis, parts, or repeat testing. We look for the in-and-out dates on each repair order rather than relying only on memory, since a vehicle may have spent time at the shop across more than one visit. We also check whether the concern was reported while the warranty was active, whether the truck was bought or leased, and who holds the original sales papers and repair records. A well-documented complaint, a clear mileage trail, and records showing what happened after each repair can make the file easier to evaluate, while missing paperwork may require more careful reconstruction.

Repair orders and VIN

Before calling, gather the repair orders from the dealer portal, email inbox, glove box, or service folder, especially the pages showing the complaint, mileage, dates in and out, diagnosis, and parts installed. Keep the VIN from the windshield, registration, or purchase paperwork nearby so we can match the truck to the service documents. The purchase or lease agreement can show the delivery date and transaction type, while warranty letters and dealer emails may preserve the wording used when the defect was reported. If a warning light appeared, note the message, the approximate mileage, and whether the truck was towed or left at the shop.
