New Car Lemon Law in Houston, TX

We review repeat new-car defects, repair orders, warranty records, and downtime.

Call (832) 981-4988 Ask a question

A warning light that returns, a grinding noise after service, a sudden stall, or a new vehicle that has gone back to the shop more than once can change the way an owner uses the car. We review the repair history behind those problems, including the complaint written on each repair order, the technician’s findings, and whether the same fault came back after the vehicle was released.

New-car problems do not always look serious on the first visit. A screen may freeze, the steering may pull, the engine may hesitate, or the air conditioning may stop cooling only under certain conditions. When the dealer cannot duplicate a symptom, the wording on the repair order can matter just as much as the part that was replaced.

This is worth looking at now when the vehicle has repeat shop visits or remains unreliable after repair work, because the next repair order, mileage entry, and date out of service can help show the sequence of the defect. We can review the records while the complaint, warranty work, and dealership correspondence are still easier to trace.

Warning lights and repeat repairs

Owners often call us about a check-engine light that clears and returns, a dashboard warning that appears after a repair, or a vehicle that loses power while merging or accelerating. We also hear about rough shifting, brake vibration, a battery that repeatedly dies, a fuel smell, water entering the cabin, and an infotainment screen that will not connect, restart, or hold its settings.

Other calls involve a new car that will not start after sitting overnight, a loud clunk from the suspension, steering that shakes at highway speed, or a recurring overheating message. Some repair orders say “could not duplicate,” while others show a software update, replacement sensor, or adjusted component without resolving the owner’s original complaint. A stall, a warning light, and a failed screen may all sound like a vehicle that is “having problems” from the driveway, but each repair history raises different questions when we compare the symptom, diagnosis, and repair work.

Case file review

We begin a case file review by listening to the vehicle problem in the owner’s own words and identifying the first visit to the dealer for that complaint. At the first conversation, we can usually sort out the vehicle’s purchase or lease status, the general nature of the defect, whether the car is still under warranty, and whether there have been repeat repair attempts. Questions about the exact diagnosis, the time in the shop, and the sequence of repairs require the paperwork in front of us.

We then read the purchase or lease agreement, each repair order, the dates the vehicle went into and out of the shop, mileage entries, warranty documents, and correspondence with the dealer or manufacturer. We check whether the written complaint matches the concern described by the owner, whether the same component or system appears across multiple visits, and whether an invoice shows the car was held for parts, testing, or an attempted repair. If records are missing, we can identify which dealership documents, text messages, emails, or service invoices may help complete the file.

Repair attempts and downtime

The number of repair attempts and the days a vehicle was out of service are central parts of a new-car record. We look for separate visits involving the same warning light, noise, stalling event, transmission concern, electrical fault, or safety-related complaint. A vehicle can have several repair orders that describe the same defect differently, so we compare the customer concern, the technician notes, the labor line, and the parts listed on every visit.

We also examine whether each issue was written down while the warranty was running, whether the vehicle was bought or leased, and who holds the original service paperwork. A verbal report at the service lane may not appear on an invoice, while a detailed repair order can preserve the date, mileage, diagnostic code, and dealer response. Records showing repeated attempts, extended shop stays, unresolved complaints, or a pattern of related repairs can make a meaningful difference in how we assess the matter, without assuming that any particular result follows.

Repair orders and VIN

Before calling, owners can gather the repair orders from the dealer’s service folder, email receipts, or online service account, along with the purchase or lease agreement and the vehicle identification number. We look for the date in, date out, mileage, customer complaint, technician comments, and parts or software updates listed on each repair order. The VIN is usually visible near the windshield on the driver’s side, on registration documents, and in insurance records. The purchase or lease agreement can help confirm the vehicle transaction and warranty paperwork can show the coverage in place when a defect was reported. Emails, text messages, and appointment confirmations may also help connect a missed appointment, parts delay, or repeated complaint to a specific shop visit.
