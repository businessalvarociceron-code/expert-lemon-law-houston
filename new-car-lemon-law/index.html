New Car Lemon Law in Houston, TX

We review repair orders for new-car defects across Houston and nearby communities.

Call (832) 981-4988 Ask a question

We hear about a warning light that returns after a repair, a noise from the engine or transmission, a dashboard screen that quits, or a vehicle that stalls at a stoplight. The concern often grows after the same new car has gone back to the shop more than once and the repair order still describes the problem as intermittent, unable to duplicate, or awaiting a part.

We review new car lemon law matters by looking beyond the symptom itself to the service record, warranty status, and sequence of repair visits. A repeating fault is worth looking at now rather than setting aside because the repair orders, mileage readings, technician notes, and dates in and out of the shop are the documents that show how the issue developed.

Warning lights and repeat repairs

We regularly hear about a check-engine light that comes back after the dealer cleared a code, a transmission that hesitates or jolts during acceleration, brakes that grind or vibrate, and an air-conditioning system that blows warm air after a repair. Other calls involve a backup camera that goes black, a key fob that will not recognize the vehicle, a battery that repeatedly loses its charge, or a steering wheel that shakes at highway speed.

We also hear from owners whose vehicle will not start, whose engine stalls in traffic, or whose infotainment screen freezes while the rear camera or navigation system is in use. Although these complaints can sound similar from a driveway or a parking lot, we examine each repair order, diagnosis, and repeat visit separately because a warning light, a drivability problem, and an electrical failure can leave very different records.

New vehicle file review

We begin a file review by discussing the vehicle, the defect, the repair history, and the current status of the warranty. During the first conversation, we can usually establish whether the car was purchased or leased, what problem brought it to the service department, whether the issue continues, and which records are available. Questions about the exact wording on a repair order, the mileage at each visit, and the dates the vehicle remained at the shop need the paperwork in front of us.

We then read the purchase or lease agreement, repair orders, invoices, vehicle identification number, warranty booklet, and correspondence with the dealer. We compare the customer concern on each repair order with the technician's findings, parts replaced, recommended follow-up work, dates in and out of the shop, and any messages about delays or unavailable parts. If records are missing, we identify the documents that may need to be requested before we can assess the full repair timeline.

Repair attempts, downtime, warranty

We look closely at the number of repair attempts for the same fault or closely related fault. A vehicle may have several shop visits, but the repair orders matter because they show whether each visit involved the same warning light, the same engine noise, the same starting problem, or an entirely different component. Notes such as “could not duplicate,” “software update performed,” “part on order,” or “condition returned” can be important when we place the visits in sequence.

We also review the days the vehicle was out of service, whether each visit was documented, and whether the defect was reported while warranty coverage was running. The purchase or lease agreement can clarify how the vehicle was acquired, while the service department records may show who retained the car, what mileage was recorded, and whether a loaner or alternate transportation was discussed. A matter can be harder to evaluate when the repair history exists only in memory, so we focus on the written record rather than assumptions about what happened at the shop.

Repair orders and VIN

Before calling, we suggest placing the repair orders together in date order and checking that each one shows the vehicle identification number, mileage, customer complaint, and service date. We also review the purchase or lease agreement because it usually identifies the vehicle, delivery date, and financing or lease information. Warranty booklets, service invoices, text messages, emails, and dealer correspondence can help fill in a gap when a repair order does not show the date the vehicle was returned. We recommend noting the dates the car went into and came out of the shop, especially where the vehicle stayed for parts, diagnosis, or repeated testing.
