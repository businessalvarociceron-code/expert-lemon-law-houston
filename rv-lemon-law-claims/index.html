RV Lemon Law Claims in Houston, TX

We review repeat RV defects, repair orders, warranty records, and downtime.

Call (832) 981-4988 Ask a question

Most RV owners first take the vehicle back to the dealer, explain the problem, and give the repair department a chance to fix it. That is a sensible first step when a slide-out will not extend, a generator stops running, a warning light appears, or water reaches a cabinet after a storm. A repair order creates a record of the complaint, the technician's inspection, and the work performed.

The problem is that an RV can spend repeated visits in the shop without the same defect being corrected. A technician may find no active code, replace one component, or describe a noise as normal, while the owner continues to experience the same issue on the road or at a campsite. The repair order may also use language that does not fully describe what happened to the leveling system, refrigerator, roof seal, or electrical panel.

When the same RV concern keeps returning, this is worth looking at now rather than leaving the paperwork in a drawer. Warranty status, repair dates, mileage entries, and the sequence of shop visits can become harder to reconstruct when a repair order is missing or a dealer record is incomplete.

Warning lights and slide-outs

We hear from RV owners about a check-engine warning light that returns after service, a slide-out that sticks or will not retract, and leveling jacks that stop working once the vehicle is parked. Other calls involve a generator that shuts down under load, a refrigerator that loses cooling, or an air-conditioning unit that quits during a trip. Water intrusion around a roof seam, window, or storage compartment is another recurring complaint because the visible stain may appear after the original repair visit.

Some RV defects affect driving, while others make the living area unusable or create concern about electrical wiring, moisture, or propane-related equipment. We look beyond the label on a repair order to compare the owner's description with the technician notes, parts listed, and return visits. A warning light and a slide-out failure may both sound like a simple service visit from the driveway, but the repair history, component involved, and written records can point to very different issues.

RV file review

We begin an RV file review by discussing the vehicle, the recurring defect, and where the RV has been serviced. In the first conversation, we can usually establish whether the RV was purchased or leased, identify the general warranty concern, and note the repair visits the owner remembers. We also ask whether the RV is currently at a dealer, whether it can be safely used, and whether any correspondence has been exchanged with the dealer or manufacturer.

The paperwork allows us to check the story against the record. We review the purchase or lease agreement, repair orders, dates the RV entered and left the shop, mileage at each visit, warranty documents, invoices, photographs, text messages, emails, and correspondence with the dealer. We compare the stated complaint on each repair order with the diagnosis, parts replacement, and closure notes, then look for repeat descriptions of the same system or condition.

Some questions can be answered from the first call, but the strength of a potential matter often depends on what the documents actually say. If records are held by a dealer, service center, lender, or owner, we can identify which documents are relevant before moving forward with a fuller review.

Repair attempts, downtime, warranty

Repeated repair attempts are an important part of an RV review. We examine whether the shop saw the same complaint more than once, whether a repair order identifies the same component, and whether a technician closed the ticket as repaired before the problem returned. A slide-out motor, control module, roof seal, engine sensor, or leveling jack can produce related complaints across several repair visits even when the wording on the paperwork changes.

Days when the RV was at a dealer or service center also matter because the dates in and out of the shop show how long the vehicle was unavailable for use. We compare those dates with work orders, appointment messages, parts-delay notes, and invoices. A vehicle that sat waiting for a component may present a different record than one that was returned after a same-day inspection.

We also check whether the defect was reported while warranty coverage was running and whether the repair order reflects that report. The purchase or lease agreement, warranty booklet, VIN, delivery date, and mileage entries help place the repair history in context. Whether the RV was bought or leased and who retains the repair records can affect what we need to review, so a complete paper trail is more useful than a memory of the shop visits alone.

Repair orders and VIN

Before calling, we suggest locating the repair orders for every visit involving the recurring RV problem, including visits where the dealer found no fault or did not replace a part. Those documents are often in the service folder, emailed appointment confirmations, dealer portal, or copies kept in the RV's glove box.

We also ask owners to have the VIN available from the registration, insurance card, purchase agreement, or label near the driver's area. The purchase or lease agreement can show the delivery date and transaction type, while the warranty booklet or warranty page may identify the applicable coverage.

Check the dates in and out of the shop and the mileage written on each repair order, especially when the RV remained at the service center for parts or further diagnosis. Keep emails, text messages, and correspondence with the dealer that describe the same warning light, leak, slide-out, generator, or electrical problem.

If a repair order uses a short description that does not match the actual defect, make a note of what occurred and when it returned.
