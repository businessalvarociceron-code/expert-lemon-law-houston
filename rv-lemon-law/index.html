RV Lemon Law in Houston, TX

We review RV repair records and repeat defects for Houston-area owners.

Call (832) 981-4988 Ask a question

When an RV starts leaking, losing power, showing a warning light, or refusing to operate a major system, taking it back to the dealer or repair shop is the sensible first step. A technician needs an opportunity to inspect the defect, run diagnostic equipment, and write a repair order. Many RV problems do have a straightforward repair, particularly when a loose connection, failed seal, or damaged component is found during the first visit.

The problem changes when the same concern returns after a repair order says it was addressed, or when each shop visit produces a different explanation for the same symptom. An RV can involve the chassis, plumbing, electrical system, slide-out mechanism, roof seals, generator, and living-area equipment, so a repeat defect can be difficult to trace from a driveway conversation alone. The paperwork from each visit often tells a clearer story than a service advisor's summary.

A recurring RV defect is worth looking at now rather than setting aside, especially when the vehicle has been unavailable for trips, the warranty period is still relevant, or the repair history is spreading across several shops. We review RV records for owners in Houston, West University Place, Southside Place, and Bellaire to see whether the documented visits and reported defects warrant a closer legal review.

Water leaks and warning lights

Owners commonly call about a roof or wall leak that returns after resealing, water collecting near a slide-out, or damp flooring that appears after rain or a wash. Another frequent complaint is a dashboard warning light that comes back after the RV has been scanned and released from the shop. Some owners report a refrigerator, air conditioner, leveling system, generator, or slide-out that stops working intermittently and cannot be duplicated during the appointment.

We also hear about RVs that lose power on the road, overheat, pull to one side, vibrate at highway speed, or develop a brake, steering, or transmission concern after a prior repair. A repair order may use broad language such as “checked” or “could not duplicate,” while the owner's notes describe the same noise, leak, or shutdown occurring again under particular conditions.

A leak, warning light, and no-start condition can all sound like a single repeat problem from the driveway, but the repair orders may show very different components, reported dates, and shop responses. We separate those records so the issue is evaluated by what was actually reported and repaired, not only by the label used during a phone call.

RV case file review

We begin an RV case file review by listening to the repair history in sequence: what first went wrong, where the RV went for service, what the technician wrote down, and what happened after it was returned. During that first conversation, we can usually clarify the vehicle type, whether it was purchased or leased, the general nature of the defect, and whether the concern has appeared on more than one repair order.

We then gather and read the purchase or lease agreement, warranty documents, repair orders, invoices, work authorizations, tow records when applicable, and correspondence with the dealer or service department. We compare the customer's reported complaint with the technician's diagnosis, the listed repair, the date the RV entered the shop, the date it was released, and the mileage recorded at each visit.

The paperwork is especially important when one repair facility handled a chassis issue while another worked on a roof seal, appliance, electrical circuit, or slide-out. We check whether the same fault was described in different words, whether the repair order identifies a part or only a symptom, and whether the reported issue appears while warranty coverage was still running. A complete file lets us assess the record without relying on memory alone.

Repair attempts and shop days

The number of repair attempts matters because repeat visits can show whether the same RV defect remained after a repair. We look for matching language across repair orders, such as repeated water intrusion at the same wall, a warning light returning after a reset, or a leveling system that repeatedly fails to extend or retract. Even where the wording changes, the date, mileage, symptom, and work performed can connect the visits.

Days out of service also matter because an RV may be unavailable while a shop waits for a part, conducts diagnostics, performs a repair, or rechecks the same complaint. The dates in and out of the shop should be preserved on each repair order. A handwritten calendar, campsite cancellation record, or email arranging service can help explain a gap when the repair order is incomplete.

We also examine whether the defect was reported while the warranty was running, whether the RV was bought or leased, and who holds the service records. A documented complaint is easier to evaluate than an issue that was discussed only by phone, but missing paperwork does not automatically end the review. The strength of a matter depends on the particular repair history, warranty record, and vehicle documents together.

Repair orders and VIN

Before calling, gather every repair order for the RV, including records from dealer service, chassis service, roof work, appliance repair, and roadside towing if those documents relate to the same concern. Check that each order shows the complaint you gave the service advisor, the date the RV entered and left the shop, the mileage, and the repair or diagnosis written by the technician. Keep the purchase or lease agreement nearby, along with the warranty booklet or warranty correspondence. The VIN can usually be found on the registration, insurance card, purchase paperwork, and the vehicle identification label. If you kept emails, text messages, photographs of leaks, or videos of a warning light, retain them with the matching repair order.
