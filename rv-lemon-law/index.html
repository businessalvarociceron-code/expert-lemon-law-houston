RV Lemon Law in Houston, TX

We review RV defects, repair orders, warranty reports, and shop records.

Call (832) 981-4988 Ask a question

We see an RV concern become more than a frustrating trip interruption when the same malfunction returns after a shop visit, a dashboard warning appears again, or a component fails before another planned departure. At that point, the repair order, mileage reading, complaint description, and date reported can matter as much as the symptom itself.

As time passes, we may have to reconstruct the sequence of shop visits from incomplete invoices, faded text messages, or a service advisor's short notes. It can also become harder to show when an owner first reported a slide-out, roof leak, engine warning, electrical fault, or appliance failure while warranty coverage was still in place.

We think an RV record with repeated repairs or lost travel time is worth looking at now rather than being set aside, because the connection between each defect, each repair order, and each warranty report is specific to the vehicle's history.

Warning lights and water leaks

We regularly hear that the check-engine light keeps returning after a service appointment, that a dashboard warning remains on, or that the RV loses power while towing or climbing. Owners also tell us that a roof seam, window frame, plumbing fitting, or slide-out seal keeps letting water into the cabin after attempted repairs.

Other calls involve a slide-out that will not extend or retract, leveling jacks that stop working, a refrigerator that loses cooling, or a generator that will not stay running. We also review complaints about batteries draining, outlets failing, air conditioning shutting off, and brake or steering problems recorded during a trip.

We separate these complaints by the affected system, the repair history, and the language on the service ticket. A warning light, water leak, and slide-out failure may all sound like an RV that is unreliable from the driveway, but we examine each record as a different problem with its own repair trail.

Case file review

We begin a case file review by discussing the RV type, the main defect, whether the vehicle was purchased or leased, and where the repairs were performed. During that first conversation, we can usually clarify the basic timeline, the recurring complaint, and which documents are already available.

We then read the purchase or lease agreement, repair orders, invoices, warranty booklet, and correspondence with the dealer or service department. We compare the dates the RV went into the shop with the dates it came back out, the mileage at each visit, the owner's written complaint, and the technician's stated diagnosis or repair.

We also look for gaps between what was reported and what appeared on the repair order. If a water intrusion complaint, chassis warning, appliance issue, or slide-out fault was discussed but not written down, we identify that difference and determine which service records, emails, or text messages may help complete the file.

Repair attempts, downtime, warranty

We look closely at the number of repair attempts for the same defect. A series of repair orders can show whether the shop addressed one recurring condition, such as a leak at the same roof area, or whether the paperwork describes separate conditions involving different components.

We also consider the days the RV was out of service and what the shop records say happened during that time. A repair order that lists parts on backorder, repeated diagnostic work, or an unresolved fault can carry different weight from an invoice showing a single completed adjustment.

Warranty timing is another central record. We check whether the owner reported the problem while the warranty was running, whether the service department documented that report, and whether the repair ticket identifies coach, chassis, electrical, plumbing, or appliance work. We also review whether the RV was bought or leased and who holds the original agreement, warranty papers, and shop records.

These factors do not turn on one line of an invoice alone. We assess the full repair history to see whether the documents support further review, which records are missing, and how clearly the timeline connects the defect to each service visit.

Repair orders and VIN

Before calling, we suggest locating every repair order, including tickets from the RV dealer, chassis service center, and appliance or generator shop. We recommend checking the purchase or lease agreement and warranty packet, which are often stored in the delivery folder, finance folder, or RV document binder. We use the VIN from the registration, insurance card, or lower windshield area to match records to the correct vehicle. We also review the dates in and out of each shop visit, the mileage shown on every repair order, and the complaint written by the service advisor. Keep emails, text messages, and correspondence with the dealer where possible, especially if a repair appointment was delayed or a defect returned after pickup.
